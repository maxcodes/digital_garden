The biggest change in how I use AI wasn't finding a better prompt. It was
expecting it to do useful work, then teaching it what good work looks like.

## How it started

My first real "aha" moment with [Claude Code](https://claude.ai/code) was
[cooking with my mom](https://github.com/maxcodes/cooking-with-claude/tree/main/examples/christmas-2025).
We planned two four-course meals and a turkey. The agent could read our recipes,
adjust portions, make shopping lists, and save our preferences.

It wasn't just answering questions. It was working with us, in our files, and
keeping what we'd figured out. I started applying the same approach to software
and everyday tasks.

The tools keep changing. These habits have been more useful than any one tool.

## Five principles

### 1. Tell it to use its brain

When an answer feels obvious or shallow, I say **"use your brain."**

I mean: think harder, question the approach, and try something more creative.
Tell it where the first attempt fell short. The phrase isn't magic; refusing
to settle for the first plausible answer is the point.

### 2. The third time, delegate it

Notice what you do each day. **The third time you do something, ask an agent
to do it for you.**

Give it the real task and the context it needs. Start with one recurring
annoyance, not a plan to automate your entire life.

### 3. Set a high bar

Expect a lot. Show it examples of good work. When its work isn't good enough,
explain why.

"This is too vague" helps. "Compare these three options, show the costs, and
recommend one" helps more. Give it something concrete to improve against.

### 4. Make the lesson stick

When it finally gets something right, say: **"Always do it this way from now on."**

Ask it to save the method in its instructions or a reusable workflow. Don't
assume a correction in one conversation will survive into the next. The point
is to stop teaching the same lesson twice.

### 5. Give it a separate reviewer

Ask it to rate its work and keep improving until it earns **9.5/10**. Ideally,
do this in a separate session that didn't produce the work.

Give the reviewer the original request, examples, and the actual result. Ask
what falls short and have the agent fix it. The score is a target, not proof:
check whether the result works.

## OpenClaw and Rita

I also wanted to delegate things away from my keyboard.
[OpenClaw](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw), formerly Clawdbot, lets me
talk to an agent through WhatsApp while it works with tools and files on my
computer.

My assistant is **Rita**. I send her messages or voice notes; she helps with
research, scheduling, cooking, and projects. She keeps notes on preferences and
ongoing work so we can pick up where we left off.

The useful part isn't having another chat window. It's being able to ask for
something while walking and come back to work that's been done.

## What we're building: Cliq

At Recurrente, we're building [Cliq](https://www.recurrente.com/cliq): an AI
assistant in WhatsApp that does things for you, like paying bills, buying
something at the pharmacy, or booking appointments.

**It asks for your approval before moving money.**

It's the same idea applied to everyday errands: ask for an outcome, not a list
of instructions you still have to carry out yourself.
